# Specialization includes the prompt contract

A writing model selected for an optional local slot was trained for story completion rather than instruction-following chat. Its publisher's native format therefore mattered as much as its parameter count. A narrow local adapter converted a premise, requested length and the previous ending into that format, while the existing application handled saved conversations and streaming. Unsupported tool requests remained visible errors.

The first accelerated co-resident load failed: driver allocation trouble stalled management probes, and watchdogs stopped both established assistants along with the candidate. Recovery restored both assistants and verified actual replies, but it did not preserve their original processes or prove interrupted jobs unaffected. A system-memory estimate had not captured the loading risk. The final candidate ran on CPU without requesting accelerator devices, retaining resource safeguards. A clean stop, fresh-process restart and overlapping real replies qualified that bounded alternative.

The application initially added built-in tools automatically. A model-specific capability setting corrected the mismatch without changing other models or pretending that the writer could use tools. A real browser then generated a short neutral scene, saved it and recovered it after refresh. Repeated submission and narrow-screen layout checks passed. Earlier conversation and authentication records were compared against a consistent snapshot; failed test conversations were retained rather than erased.

The result is useful for story premises and bounded continuations, with slower CPU execution. It is not evidence of universal writing quality, full-window recall, refusal behaviour, reboot recovery or endurance. The engineering lesson is that a specialist model needs its training contract carried through the whole application, and that recovery must be reported separately from uninterrupted preservation. A safer final mode does not erase an earlier outage.
